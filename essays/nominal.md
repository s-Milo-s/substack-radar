Nominal, to my understanding, is trying to be the Palantir of hardware testing, with an eye on moving into the operations space. This is a good goal, but for the uninitiated, we should first explain the problem they are trying to solve.
The Problem
Imagine you want to test your new sleek F-22 Raptor, or more commonly, a particular component like the engine, or you want to stress test the frame of the aircraft. For anything meant to carry people, there are millions of tests of every component to make sure it meets an approved standard. This is because, one, human lives depend on it; two, national security. Order those as you see fit. This extends to products that are not critical to human life and national defense as well. Your customer would hate you if your blender broke. With that out of the way, we have our setup for the problem. How do you test different systems with a variety of different sensors? Think of one that measures heat and another that measures tolerance. All these systems have different manufacturers who, being the greedy bastards they are, try to lock you into the slop fest of vendor software they slap on top of it. It's called vendor lock-in. That being said, the industry has made some progress toward a standard. The first problem is getting all these to work in unison, and the second is exporting the data to see whether the test actually worked in a reasonable way. Success of your test isn't determined by the outcome of any one sensor. You need a way to trigger and manage all these sensors at once and monitor them in real time through a coherent pane of glass. Further, you need a way to unify this data in a compliant manner, visualize it on the fly, and compare it to benchmarks, aka historical data. You also need a way to collect this data in a central location, run longer-term analysis on it, and eventually produce custom reports for your customers and stakeholders that prove whether what you did actually worked and what needs to change. That's the problem, it's messy, and I'm not even getting into the weeds yet. Every Excel file that you export and have to line up by hand costs time, and as we all know, time is money. A slower review means fewer test cycles, and fewer cycles means the program ships later. Worse, these artifacts are stored in a SharePoint drive on the computer of some 60-year-old engineer who is still actively posting on Facebook. You have no verified way to look back at history. Even if you are fortunate enough to have a robust internal process for this, managing it in-house is a massive cost center. Data engineers aren't cheap, and neither is building in-house software to do this properly.
The Model
So what is Nominal doing to solve this billion-dollar question? I would say basically ripping off Palantir, except focusing on one slice of an industry instead of going for the whole pie. The work starts with the forward-deployed folks who embed with your company and instrument your test collection software, pulling it all into one location, as well as providing write access to the test stand itself. Where this location lives is a matter of contract. It can stay with you in an air-gapped environment, or it can live in their systems, which I pray is a highly segmented and restricted environment but is still managed by them. On top of this sit two applications. The first is a desktop application that can run the test and automatically stop it based on sensor limits, alongside the hardware safety systems that protect the stand regardless. Think of it as a central operating system for your hardware testing environment. Take our F-22 example: when you test a jet engine, you bolt it to the floor, with hundreds or thousands of sensors measuring it and controls to turn the rig on and off and set its mode. Along the way, this sensor data is read into an ontology, a labeled set of streams and files that can later be searched and associated with a run. That is the magic. Then this data, which has been streaming through this supposedly well-oiled data pipeline, lands in the core application, which organizes it and lets engineers analyze it. Then it produces those reports you need to tell your boss it's time for a promo. All optimized for testing hardware. Again, this is what they claim; how much and how well it's implemented ¯(ツ)/¯.
Why Now?
This is an easy question to answer for anyone even remotely following the news. Hardware companies have become the darling of Silicon Valley and, to an extent, Wall Street as well. a16z's American Dynamism fund and JPMorgan's Security and Resiliency Initiative have been throwing money at hardware, manufacturing, and defense firms. Marc Andreessen is also now on the Defense Policy Board, which advises Pentagon leadership on strategic planning. Meaning the guys investing in this are also advising the buyer. Good for Nominal's sales pipeline, risky if the politics shift. God, who doesn't love a revolving-door oligarchy? I do lowkey mess with it because I like hardware, but that's beside the point and morally dubious.
Adding fuel to the fire, the international order is sliding toward a multipolar one where it is becoming clearer that might makes right, driving global instability and pushing up defense budgets. To tie it all off, hardware components have seen dramatic cost reductions thanks to products that drove economies of scale: first the smartphone, which drove sensor costs down, and now batteries and motors from the takeoff of the electric car. With one caveat: this economy of scale exists in China. Which brings us back to our changing world order. We (America) have been pushing onshoring, further increasing demand for new domestic factories and suppliers, each of which has to prove its parts work before a prime will buy them. Lastly, if you want to supercharge this: in the age of AI, data is among a company's most valuable assets, and the hardware companies that can access and organize their test data will separate themselves from the ones that can't. At least that's the thesis.
All this to say, there are a lot more hardware startups in the US, and every one of them has to test what they build. So the bet is that the wedge is big enough for a company devoted to just testing. Thank you for sitting through my geopolitical and economic rant.

The Moat
So what makes this company hard to beat? No one ever says dashboards. Reporting and analysis on their own are easy to copy, and plenty of tools can draw a nice chart. The real moat is control. Nominal doesn't just read data off the test stand; it runs the stand. Coordinating every sensor and control from one place is a superpower. Once your tests start and stop through Nominal, ripping it out means rewiring your whole test cell. That being said, no one is being tricked into this.
Now, the obvious question: isn't this just the vendor lock-in I was complaining about earlier? Eh, kind of. Every platform wins this way. The difference is what you're locked into. The old vendors trap you in their hardware. Nominal locks you into the layer that frees you from all of them. You're handing the hard, annoying, low-value work of integrating these systems to a specialized team. This is a cost center, after all; it doesn't add to your bottom line. That's a lock-in customers might actually choose.
The second layer is the data. Every test run adds to a labeled, searchable history in Nominal's ontology. Year three is worth more than year one, and in the age of AI, that history is the asset. You own your raw numbers, sure, but the organization is what makes them useful. Export them without the ontology and they're just numbers, hard to train or learn from.
The last layer is know-how. Because they only do testing, they get better at integrating these systems with every customer. I'd call that the weakest layer, though. Know-how walks out the door when engineers leave (caveat: recording this know-how more directly may be more possible with AI)^make into a source lol. And control cuts both ways: giving an outside startup like Nominal write access to a safety-critical test stand is a slow sell. The moat is strong once it's in place, but getting it in place takes time.
The Competition
So the natural next question I always ask is: what is everyone else doing? Is there another player? The answer is yes, but in a couple of different ways. The three main ones we will talk about are Sift, which was founded in 2022 by two former SpaceX engineers; the incumbents, led by Emerson, which acquired National Instruments (NI) in 2023; and the DIY option, which I am a big believer in.
Sift is the most direct competitor. Sift is much smaller (reportedly valued at about $274M, a fraction of Nominal's size), and it tries to unify the hardware data stack from an operational standpoint. They are looking to be your one-stop shop for all data coming off live machines. Think Datadog, but instead of server errors, we are watching satellites and reporting their status as the data comes in. Sift seeks to be the screen in the control room that gives you visibility into your fleet. In my view, this is where the crossover comes in. In many cases, the sensors you plug into while in operation are the same ones you used in test, so it's conceivable that these two will step on each other's toes, or we will see an acquisition in this space at some point. Both were founded the same year, starting from opposite ends and heading toward each other.
The next competitor is Emerson, which bought NI, the maker of some of the most common test hardware out there. If you walk into a test cell, odds are something in it runs on NI. NI has worked to build a cohesive platform for users to view this data, called SystemLink, and is now adding AI on top of it with a feature they call Nigel AI. That being said, it's built around NI's own devices, so it's not a true unifier. The real threat isn't that it's better than Nominal; it's that it's already there. For a prime that already owns a room full of NI equipment, adding SystemLink is an easy "good enough" that doesn't require bringing in a new vendor. And NI isn't alone. Curtiss-Wright owns flight test, HBK owns structural testing, Siemens owns automotive. Each owns its slice, and each benefits from the vendor lock-in I complained about earlier.
The last competitor worth watching is DIY. With the proliferation of AI, engineers can do things in weeks that would have been a year-long timeline back in the stone age of hand-stitched code. It is conceivable for a cracked group of in-house engineers to build a custom test suite and platform better suited to a prime's or mid-size manufacturer's needs than Nominal can provide. SpaceX did exactly this, which is where Sift's founders came from. That being said, AI cuts both ways: the same tools that let an in-house team move fast let Nominal build and deploy faster too. And people, especially in the middle market, tend to outsource this stuff even if the ability is there, because cracked engineers are extremely expensive.
So Nominal's real fight isn't one competitor. It's Sift coming from the other side, incumbents good enough to stall a switch, and customers who can build it themselves.
The Verdict
I think about verdicts in a couple of ways: one from the view of a traditional investor, the other from that of an employee joining now.
First, let's look at it as an investor. I think this is a very defensible business that will keep growing at a steady clip, thanks to the geopolitical tailwinds, economies of scale, and the institutional knowledge built from repeating the same implementation pattern. Advanced data and test management is not easy, and a consultancy/SaaS hybrid devoted to it is a genuine niche. Long term, I think this business helps a lot of smaller startups scale fast.
The bigger question is the move into operations, because that's what the $3B price is betting the house on. IMO, they'll make headway, but they won't dominate. Operational data is extremely sensitive. It's how your fleet actually performs in the field, and for a prime, that's the crown jewels. Large orgs will keep a lot of this homegrown. So I think Nominal's operations market is bound to small and mid-size companies.
So as an investor: great business, full price. At $3B, you're paying for an expansion that I think lands, just smaller than the pitch deck says.
Now from the perspective of an employee joining now, ignoring personal and professional growth and looking at it purely in dollars: I cap this niche at roughly $5–7B. Joining at $3B, that's about 2x before the niche is full, which, by the way, I don't think they will capture completely either. Life-changing equity usually means 10x, which here means a $30B company, and that only happens if operations and AI go much bigger than I think they will. You are not joining the next Palantir. Don't delude yourself. Add in dilution from future rounds and a long wait before your "equity" is worth anything you can spend, and I wouldn't bet on it changing your life. That being said, at this stage there's a ton of room for career growth. Just join for the résumé, not the lottery ticket.
